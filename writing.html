build systems are load-bearing

jun 2026

I spent my first weeks at NVIDIA inside rapids-cmake, the layer that holds the RAPIDS stack together. It’s the kind of code most people only notice when it breaks, which is exactly what makes it interesting: every project downstream inherits its decisions about compilers, architectures, and dependency resolution, and nobody sends a thank-you note when all of that just works.

The thing I underestimated is that a build system is a distributed systems problem wearing a trench coat. Caching, invalidation, hermeticity, fan-out across targets — the vocabulary is the same. A flaky build is a consistency bug. A slow one is a latency bug. The fact that the artifact is a compiled binary instead of a query result is almost incidental.

My takeaway so far: infrastructure earns trust by being boring. Code that supports other people’s work should be the least surprising thing in their day. There’s real craft in that kind of boring, and I’d like to get better at it.

what an order book taught me about memory

jan 2026

The first version of my matching engine kept its book in a std::map per side. It was correct, it was obvious, and the profiler showed it spending most of its life chasing pointers. Every order touched nodes scattered across the heap, and the cache hit rate told the story better than any benchmark summary could.

The rewrite stored price levels in flat arrays indexed by tick, with intrusive lists for orders at each level. Same algorithm on paper — price-time priority either way — but the machine saw something completely different: predictable strides, prefetchable layouts, and allocations moved out of the hot path entirely.

The lesson wasn’t “maps are slow.” It’s that big-O reasoning stops being the dominant term long before most of us expect it to. Past that point, performance is a data-layout problem, and the only honest way to reason about it is to measure. The profiler is the code review the hardware gives you.

on ai

mar 2025

Written over spring break of freshman year, mostly to figure out what I actually believed.

Everyone in my CS classes uses AI. The interesting question isn’t whether that’s allowed — it’s what it does to the muscle you came here to build. When a model autocompletes the hard part, you don’t just skip the typing; you skip the part where the problem pushes back. That resistance is the entire workout.

So my rule for now: think first, prompt second. I write the approach down — actual sentences, sometimes on paper — before I ask a model anything. If I can’t explain the shape of the solution, I’m not ready to delegate the details. The model is a power tool, and power tools are for people who can already hold the material.
